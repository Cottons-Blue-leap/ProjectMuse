Attached is Dante Gabriel Rossetti's "Proserpine" (1874), a Pre-Raphaelite oil painting: a melancholic woman in a deep teal-blue silk gown stands against a dark wall, holding a bitten pomegranate at her chest, her other hand clasping that wrist; an ivy sprig hangs beside her, a soft shaft of gray light glows on the wall, and a small incense burner sits at the lower left.

Edit this painting so the woman becomes Hatsune Miku — while the ENTIRE image still looks like Rossetti's original oil. Two requirements are equally critical and must BOTH hold:

1) TONE: fully preserve the painting's tone and manner — deep jewel-toned Pre-Raphaelite oil, smooth glazed brushwork, somber light. Imagine Rossetti himself painted a portrait of Hatsune Miku: render her entirely in his OIL technique — smooth painterly modeling, deep palette, softly painted shading. NOT flat cel shading, NOT hard anime outlines, NOT bright cartoon coloring. She is an oil-painted figure, never a cartoon pasted on top. Every detail on her must belong to 1874 — NOTHING modern anywhere in the painting.

2) EXPRESSION — the heart of this image: the wary, watchful gaze of the original Proserpine, with quiet tears.
- Her eyes are OPEN and ALERT, turned in a sidelong gaze toward something far beyond the frame (toward the shaft of light) — the guarded, watchful look of a captive who senses she is not free, with quiet grief and longing beneath it.
- TEARS, subtle: her lower eyelids are moist and faintly glistening, and one thin tear has just welled over, beginning to slide down her cheek — delicate, painted in the same soft oil technique, catching a tiny glint of light. The tears carry the sorrow.
- Because the tears carry the sorrow, keep the rest of the face composed: brows soft and only very slightly drawn — do NOT knit or twist them into open anguish; lips closed, soft, unsmiling. She is NOT sobbing, NOT anguished, NOT gloomy-faced — a captive girl, alert and silent, with tears she does not acknowledge.
- Absolutely NOT expressionless, NOT a blank serene face, NOT sleepy, NOT downcast eyes, NOT a smile.

She must also be unmistakably Hatsune Miku, her iconic features rendered in oil:
- Long teal twin-tails tied in two tails — replacing the dark wavy hair but framing her face with the same volume; slightly lighter than the gown, never neon.
- The twin-tails are bound with small, dark, muted silk or velvet bands in a deep tone that almost disappears into the hair — period-appropriate for an 1874 painting. Absolutely NO modern hair accessories: no plastic clips, no Miku's angular red-black clip holders, no bright ribbons, no bows that look contemporary.
- A youthful, girlish round face — clearly a young teenage girl, NOT the original mature woman: soft round cheeks, a small nose and a small soft mouth, delicate East-Asian features (drop the original's strong jaw and heavy lips).
- Noticeably large, soft eyes with a teal tint — larger than a realistic portrait but painted softly, carrying the wary, tearful gaze described above. Alive, never a blank doll.
- Slender, petite build.

Keep everything else exactly as Rossetti painted it: her exact pose (three-quarter view, head turned, the bitten pomegranate at her chest, one hand clasping the other wrist), the deep teal-blue silk gown with its silvery highlights, the ivy, the shaft of light, the incense burner, the dark wall, the whole composition. Keep the original aspect ratio. Add no new text or border; the painting's own small inscription panels may remain.
